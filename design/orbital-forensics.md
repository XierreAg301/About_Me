# Orbital Forensics

Orbital Forensics treats darkness as an active material: a vast field in which every illuminated edge has evidentiary weight. Space is not filled; it is calibrated. Spheres, trajectories, and nodes appear as precisely measured interruptions in the void, with enough negative space for the eye to understand distance before it reads information. Every interval should feel meticulously crafted, the result of repeated optical adjustment rather than a convenient grid.

Form follows a forensic logic. The globe is the primary specimen, clouds are transitional matter, and the node constellation is the decoded record. Circles, radial lines, orbital paths, and sparse coordinate marks communicate the system visually before words explain it. The work should reward scrutiny: tiny repetitions and restrained technical marks suggest a master-level instrument assembled with painstaking attention.

The palette is nearly monochrome—true black, mineral white, smoked steel—with amber used only when the system asks for human action. Color is therefore not decoration but state. Materials shift from atmospheric translucency to hard luminous linework as the visitor moves closer to the portfolio. Every glow, grain, and reflection must appear intentionally tuned by someone at the top of their field.

Scale creates the narrative rhythm. The first globe is monumental and silent; the cloud descent overwhelms the frame; the final node map becomes intimate and analytical. Motion is sparse but consequential, using one continuous spatial idea rather than many competing effects. The result should feel labored over for countless hours, with each transition preserving orientation and visual continuity.

Typography behaves like an annotation layer. It is small, clinical, and rare until a node is decoded, when one strong title and a measured column of evidence appear beside the object. Text never competes with the 3D scene. Composition carries the story, and language only names what the viewer has already discovered.

The quiet reference is a flight recorder recovered from an unknown orbit: incomplete, exacting, and strangely personal. Each portfolio section is evidence connected through a navigable celestial system. The finished work must feel less like a website template and more like a painstakingly restored scientific artifact.
